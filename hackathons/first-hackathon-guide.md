# First Hackathon Guide

A practical guide for students participating in their first hackathon.

---

## Before the hackathon

### 1. Choose the right hackathon

For your first time, pick a hackathon that:
- Is beginner-friendly (look for mentions of mentorship or beginner tracks)
- Has a theme you are interested in
- Is online (less pressure than offline)
- Has a longer duration (24-48 hours is manageable)

Good first hackathons include Devfolio community events, MLH Localhost, or college-level fests.

### 2. Form a team

- 3-4 members is a good size for most hackathons
- Mix of skills: at least one developer, one designer, one person comfortable presenting
- Team members can be from different colleges or backgrounds
- If you do not have a team, many hackathons have team-formation channels on Slack/Discord

### 3. Prepare your tools

Before the event starts:
- Install and test all required software (IDEs, frameworks, tools)
- Set up version control (GitHub/GitLab)
- Prepare templates for common components (authentication, basic frontend)
- Have API keys ready if you plan to use external services
- Make sure your laptop is charged and your internet connection is stable

### 4. Research the problem or theme

- If problem statements are announced beforehand, read all of them
- If there is an open theme, brainstorm ideas with your team
- Check what technologies the hackathon encourages or requires

---

## During the hackathon

### 5. Start simple

- Do not over-engineer. Focus on a working prototype first
- Split work: frontend, backend, database, presentation — assign clear roles
- Use existing templates, libraries, and APIs rather than building from scratch

### 6. Manage your time

The typical flow for a 24-hour hackathon:

| Time | Activity |
|---|---|
| First 2 hours | Finalize idea, plan architecture, divide tasks |
| Next 6-8 hours | Core development |
| Next 6-8 hours | Complete features, fix bugs |
| Last 4 hours | Polish UI, prepare presentation, rehearse pitch |
| Last 1 hour | Final submission |

- Take short breaks. Sleep if it is a multi-day event.
- Eat properly. Most hackathons provide meals.

### 7. Use mentors

- Mentors are there to help you. Ask questions early, not at the last minute
- Share your progress and blockers with mentors
- Mentors can help with technical issues, idea refinement, and pitch coaching

### 8. Prepare your pitch

Most hackathons require a final presentation:
- Problem statement and why it matters (1-2 minutes)
- Your solution and how it works (2-3 minutes)
- Demo of your working prototype (2-3 minutes)
- Future scope and impact (1 minute)
- Keep it under 5-7 minutes total

---

## After the hackathon

- Submit your project on time, even if it is incomplete
- Upload your code to GitHub with a good README
- Add the project to your resume and portfolio
- Connect with people you met on LinkedIn
- Review what you learned and what you would do differently

---

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Picking a complex idea that cannot be built in limited time
- Not dividing work clearly among team members
- Spending too much time on UI at the cost of functionality
- Ignoring mentors and judges
- Not practicing the pitch
- Submitting at the last second (technical issues happen)

---

## What to bring to an offline hackathon

- Laptop and charger
- Extension cord (outlets are always scarce)
- Earphones
- Water bottle
- Hoodie or jacket (venues can be cold)
- Notebook and pen
- Personal toiletries

---

## Mindset

- You will probably not win your first hackathon. That is fine.
- The goal is to learn, build something, and meet people.
- Even an incomplete project is a portfolio item.
- Every hackathon makes the next one easier.
